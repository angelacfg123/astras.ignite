A Silent Archive Wandering Mars

It all started during the year 2018, whereas InSight Lander dispatched near Mars’ equator on the western side of a flat, smooth plain called Elysium Planitia. While the red planet's structure had been shifting ever since it formed 4.5 billion years ago, InSight lander was programmed to observe and study the innermost part of it. Its job was important, as it was the key to all questions about the early formation of rocky planets in our inner solar system; Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. More than 4 billion years ago, as well as rocky exoplanets, InSight also measured tectonic activity and meteorite impacts on Mars.

The lander used cutting edge instruments, to delve deep beneath the surface and seek the fingerprints of the processes that formed the terrestrial planets. Each of its scientific instruments had a precious goal to achieve. First was SEIS (Seismic Experiment for Interior Structure). Unlike any other instrument, this instrument detected a sensitive seismometer deployed to the Martian surface that recorded over 1,300 marsquakes, offering help for scientists to map the thickness of the crust, mantle structure, and core size. Then comes the HP3 (Heat Flow and Physical Properties Probe), a self-hammering heat probe, also known as “the mole”. Its plan was to burrow up to 5 meters deep. Last but not least, RISE (Rotation and Interior Structure Experiment). This magnificent instrument used radio wave sensors to track planet wobble and reveal data about the liquid core of Mars.

After years of analyzing the wonderful, expansive structure of Mars–the lander was unexpectedly disrupted by its own machines. During the operation of the lander, out of the blue, InSight’s power depleted completely. Right away, all the batteries had run out, causing it to slowly dry and lose its main function; keeping the spacecraft from operating. The lander was unable to transmit data. With the situation happening, NASA couldn’t pinpoint the exact minute it shut down.

Over the years, NASA had previously decided to declare the mission over if the lander missed two communication attempts. The agency will continue to listen to rare signal appearances from the lander for further backup. The last recorded communication between InSight and planet earth was not too long ago from that time. It had been heard during the 15th of December.

“I watched the launch and landing of this mission, and while saying goodbye to a spacecraft is always sad, the fascinating science InSight conducted is cause for celebration,” said Thomas Zurbuchen, administrator of NASA’s Science Mission Directorate in Washington, as the journey to Mars was about to step into its final destination. “The seismic data alone from this Discovery Program mission offers tremendous insights not just into Mars but other rocky bodies, including Earth.”

The amazing InSight lander made such impacts, it was outside of everyone's imagination. From the start, it helped scientists determine the age of the planet's surface and data from the seismometer surprisingly provided scientists to study the planet's compositions.

As for the Phoenix Lander, it had operated long before the InSight Lander existed. On May 25, 2008 Phoenix–an uninhabited space probe had landed on Green Valley, located in the depths of Mars. The lander was programmed to study the geological history of water, which was the key to unlock hidden stories of climate change that were buried hundreds of years ago. Phoenix’s instruments had potential to uncover information on the geological and biological history of the Martian Arctic.

Just as the primary mission was operated, Phoenix had experienced severe malfunctions. Without a clear warning, the craft exceeded its expected operational lifetime by a little over two months before succumbing to the frigid and thickly covered clouds of the advancing Martian winter. Inside Phoenix’s machine, all the batteries started drying up, causing it to be unable to survive in the harsh, cold part of Mars. Researchers had put effort so the lander would survive into the Martian winter, in hopes of witnessing polar ice developing around it, formed by solid carbon dioxide ice.

Without a trace, Phoenix lander sent its last brief signal on November 2, 2008, which was about six months after the landing. Right after that, it ended communications between the NASA program. From the start, it was never designed to survive the polar winter or return to Earth. However, before shutting down–Pheonix confirmed underground water ice, observed the falling snow, detected perchlorates and calcium carbonate in the Martian soil. The efforts made by Phoenix were not thrown to waste, but its data and observations were rightfully used by NASA researchers from around the world.

Straight after the InSight and Phoenix lander was announced to the public that it had successfully reached its final journey to Mars’ surface, it spanned excitement over its unique scientific mission, feelings of anxiety during a launch delay, and widespread celebration across its operational life. InSight Lander had become the first outer space robotic explorer to study in-depth the “inner space” of Mars: Its crust, mantle, and core. Meanwhile, Phoenix Lander became a scientific success and made a brand new record; becoming the first spacecraft to physically touch and confirm water ice on Mars while outlasting its planned lifespan by more than two months.
